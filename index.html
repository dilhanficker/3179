Chapter 1 · Domestic adoption

How quickly are Australian businesses embracing AI?

From barely one-in-five organisations in 2017, AI use in Australia has surged to over three-quarters by 2024 — a trajectory sharply accelerated by two inflection points: the disruption of COVID-19 and the 2022 release of ChatGPT.

Australian enterprise AI adoption rate, 2017–2024
Source: KPMG Australia AI Pulse Survey 2024 · kpmg.com/au
Two turning points. The COVID-19 pandemic (2020) forced accelerated digital transformation across Australian industry, pushing adoption from 34% to 46% in two years. The ChatGPT launch in late 2022 triggered a second surge — Australian businesses jumped from 55% to 67% adoption in a single year, the fastest rate of growth in the dataset.

Yet adoption alone does not tell the whole story. Which industries are leading, and which are being left behind? The chart below shows that Financial Services and Technology are well ahead — adoption above 78% — while Agriculture (27%) and Government (38%) are at risk of missing the productivity gains their private-sector peers are already capturing.

Chapter 2 · Geography of digital talent

Where are Australia's ICT workers concentrated?

AI capability is not evenly distributed across Australia. The density of ICT professionals — the workforce most likely to build, deploy, and govern AI systems — varies sharply by state and territory. This uneven geography shapes which parts of the country benefit from AI first.

ICT professionals as share of total workforce, by state and territory (2023–24)
Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics — Labour Force Survey 2023–24 · abs.gov.au
The ACT stands apart. The Australian Capital Territory records 6.1% of its workforce in ICT roles — more than four times the Northern Territory's 1.5%. This reflects the concentration of federal government technology agencies in Canberra. New South Wales (4.2%) and Victoria (3.9%) lead among the larger states, anchored by Sydney and Melbourne's technology sectors. Regional and northern Australia face the most acute digital skill shortages.

The geographic concentration of ICT talent in Sydney, Melbourne, and Canberra means that AI-driven economic gains are likely to accrue disproportionately to Australia's eastern seaboard — at least in the near term. Addressing this spatial inequality will be central to any genuinely national AI strategy.

Chapter 3 · Australia's AI market

A rapidly growing domestic market — but still a fraction of global scale

Australia's AI market has grown more than fourfold since 2020. Forecast to reach AUD 17.5 billion by 2028, it represents one of the fastest-growing technology segments in the Australian economy — though this remains a very small share of the global market.

Software dominates globally (56%). Software leads the global AI market at USD 139B, ahead of Hardware and Services (each USD 58.6B). Generative AI platforms — barely a category three years ago — already represent USD 44B. For Australian businesses, this market composition indicates where investment decisions will have the highest impact.
Chapter 4 · Global investment context

Where does Australia rank in the global AI investment race?

Private investment — the fuel of AI innovation — flows overwhelmingly to the United States. Australia's USD 1.8 billion in 2024 places it 8th globally, ahead of South Korea and Singapore, but dwarfed by the US at USD 109 billion. Understanding Australia's position requires looking at both the absolute numbers and the trajectory over time.

Private AI investment by country, 2024 (USD billions) — Australia highlighted in amber
Source: Stanford HAI AI Index Report 2025 · aiindex.stanford.edu

The chart below shows how investment has evolved from 2021 to 2024. A square-root colour scale is used deliberately: without it, the USA's dominance would render every other country's bar nearly invisible. Notice how Australia and most European nations appear at similar low intensities — the gap is structural, not cyclical.

AI private investment by country and year, 2021–2024 (USD billions)
Source: Stanford HAI AI Index Report 2025 · aiindex.stanford.edu

Colour uses a square-root scale to prevent the USA from overwhelming detail in other countries.

Australia's investment is growing — but slowly. Australia increased from USD 0.9B (2021) to USD 1.8B (2024), a 100% rise. However, the USA grew from USD 110B to USD 109B over the same period (a brief dip before the 2025 surge). The relative gap has not closed — and without a step change in both public and private AI investment, Australia risks becoming a consumer of AI rather than a producer.
Global hyperscaler AI capital expenditure, 2022–2026 (USD billions)
Source: Goldman Sachs Equity Research 2025

2026 value is a forecast (lighter bar). Hyperscalers = Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta combined.

The infrastructure race is accelerating. Global hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google, and Meta — are on track to spend USD 430 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 alone. This spending dwarfs Australia's entire annual AI market. For Australian organisations, this means world-class AI infrastructure will remain accessible via cloud — but sovereign capability requires domestic investment in talent and data.

Chapter 6 · AI readiness — Australia vs. the world

Where does Australia genuinely stand — and where must it improve?

The Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index 2025 scores countries across five dimensions. Australia performs respectably on digital infrastructure and data governance, but the gaps in AI talent and private investment are substantial — and widening relative to Singapore and the USA.

AI readiness by dimension — Australia vs. comparable nations (score: 0–100)
Source: Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index 2025 · oxfordinsights.com
Australia USA UK Singapore
Private investment is Australia's most critical gap. Australia scores just 38 out of 100 on private investment — 61 points below the USA and 17 below Singapore. The dumbbell chart makes this stark: the bar for Private Investment is the longest, meaning the spread between Australia and the leaders is the widest of any dimension. Digital infrastructure (78) and data governance (68) are relative strengths. AI talent and skills (61) is the next most urgent priority.

The good news: data governance and digital infrastructure are areas where Australia punches closer to its weight. The National AI Strategy and existing ASD/ACS frameworks provide a foundation. The challenge is converting strategic intent into funded, scalable programs that attract and retain AI talent domestically.
